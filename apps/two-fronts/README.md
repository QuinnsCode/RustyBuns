# Two Fronts

> **What it proves:** a turn-based strategy engine and three.js battles, on the ground and in orbit, built apart and joined by small JSON types. Against the AI or hotseat it runs in the page; online, each campaign is a Durable Object that keeps the turns in its SQLite. One of the [Rusty Buns](../../README.md#see-it-work) examples.

*The Wooka came with a furry ferocity unseen by the likes of man.*

The Thunder Marines landed on Doren, the forest moon, to clear out a few knee-high locals. They brought walkers, gunships and orbital strikes. The locals brought clubs, and they know every fern. Somewhere between the drop and the generator, the hunters became the hunted.

The Wooka have pushed into the galaxy, and only the Thunder Marines stand in their way. The Marines are the good guys here: black armour with white trim, full-face helmets with snorkel masks on the front, and backpacks far too big for anyone, bristling with antennas. The Wooka are round and fluffy, about knee-high, with big black eyes that have nothing behind them and clubs built to maim. The radio keeps reminding you that they are not cuddly.

*Hold the line* drops you straight into the default fight: a Thunder Marine holding the shield generator against the horde in a redwood forest. *Be the horde* plays it from the other side, as a Wooka chieftain. 🚀 holds an orbit against the Wooka fleet, picking orders round by round or flying a fighter. The campaign wraps those fights in a galactic war you have to win on two fronts. Fleets fight for the orbit of each world and armies fight for its sectors. Once a turn, you pick one battle and play it yourself as a hero: a ground battle, in a third-person fight over command posts, or a space battle for the orbit, fought by picking orders (or flown in a fighter). This builds on the plan in [notes/game-ideas/2-two-fronts.md](../../notes/game-ideas/2-two-fronts.md).

- **Who plays:** ↺ starts a campaign on any of the three maps, three ways. *Against the AI*: you're the Thunder Marines, and the Wooka AI moves when you end your turn. *Hotseat*: two players at one screen, handing it over between turns, so neither sees the other's orders. *Online*: you get a link to send whoever plays the Wooka, and you each hand in orders from your own link whenever you like. The turn resolves once both are in, and the page checks back every few seconds while it waits. Each side has its own hero, and a turn still has one hero battle: if both pick one, the Marines' pick goes first.
- **The war table:** the galaxy is a star map over nebulae. Each system is a glowing star with a name plaque and a dot per sector in its holder's colour. Its territory bulges round it, and a side's territories run together into one translucent sweep with a glowing border where it meets the enemy, hatched red when the enemy holds the orbit. Regions are dashed outlines, with a badge for their bonus that lights up for the side holding every capital in it. A bar along the top shows your credits and income, the war score, your armies, ships, regions and hero. The overview down the left lists every world you hold, with its sectors, armies and ships (click one to select it), and the next-turn button sits in the bottom corner. Armies are numbered counters and fleets numbered chevrons, a lane between two sides burns as the front line, and your orders are arrows with their count on the bend. The fog of war hides the enemy's numbers: you see who holds every world, but how many armies and ships they have there only where you have someone of your own. Elsewhere their pieces show a question mark. The panel's income line opens to break next turn's income down: sectors held, each region bonus, and the morale cut if you're losing. The panel says things with lucide icons and tooltips rather than words: send rows are an icon, a count, an arrow, a destination and a send button.
- **The galaxy:** the Thunder Marines against the Wooka, on a map you pick when you start a new campaign. The short war is eight systems in three regions, an evening's play. The full campaign is thirty systems in seven regions, over several evenings: each side's two home regions, with the High Drift, the Narrows and the Ember Belt between them. Hold every capital in a region for its bonus. Most worlds are one capital. On the short map, Kessa and Lumen, the two worlds of the Narrows, each have three sectors: a shield generator, a spaceport and a capital. Corva and Morrow, each side's way into its home region, each have an anti-ship cannon beside their capital. The full map has eight multi-sector worlds: a shipyard homeworld for each side, a shipyard in each outer middle region, shield worlds either side of the Narrows, and a cannon world guarding each way into a home region. Tarn and Umber, in the Narrows, each have a mining canyon whose ore pays as well as a capital, and Rook and Xan each have a jungle, which its holder defends as if they held the orbit. *The Horde* is sixty worlds in twelve regions, twelve columns of five, the Marines' half to the west and the Wooka's to the east, with Doren, the forest moon, as the Wooka's home. Its ground is point-symmetric like the others, but its forces aren't: the Wooka come in force, two or three armies to a world against the Marines' one or two, and the Marines hold the sky with more ships. On a map that size a side is paid under a third of what its sectors earn, so the war doesn't drown in armies. The short and full maps are point-symmetric in everything, so neither side starts with the better ground.
- **Choosing a fleet:** a war opens before turn 1 with two choices. First pick a fleet from a pool of value (a ship is worth what it costs to build, and so is an army): the *Armada* (mostly ships), the *Battle line* (the map's own mix), the *Vanguard* (the same mix, all on the front line) or the *Horde* (mostly troops), or spend the pool on ships and armies of your own. Then place it on the worlds you hold, with +/- on each world, or let it spread itself. Where it stands is the opening move: a forward fleet hits first but leaves the rear empty, a garrisoned one holds out longest. Both sides place at once and blind, and the AI picks a preset off the campaign's seed. What you don't place goes into the treasury.
- **Bombardment:** hold a world's orbit alone, with their last ship there beaten, and you can train ships on a garrison below: each kills an army before the landings (half as many through a shield generator), and ships that bombard don't sail. It can't take ground by itself: you still have to land to hold it, so winning the war from the sky is possible but slow.
- **A turn:** both sides at once, blind to each other's numbers, in six phases like a board game of territories and armies. The strip of icons at the top of the panel lights the one you're in. *Draw*: each side draws a card, the same one for both. *Place*: raise troops and ships (armies at a capital, ships at a spaceport), then play cards. *Declare*: move, and any move into the enemy's orbit or ground is an attack. The attacks are declared: each side sees where it's being attacked and from where, but not with how many, and *retreats* if it wants to. A defender can pull everything out of an attacked sector to one it holds on the same world or next door, or pull its ships back along a hyperlane, giving up the ground to keep the troops. An attacker can call an attack off and keep its troops at home. If nobody is attacked, the turn skips straight on. *Combat*: space battles come first, then landings, then ground battles. Each battle you don't play is a cannon barrage of dice: each side fires up to three guns a round, its leader rolling a d8 and the rest d6s, highest against highest, and ties go to the attacker. Pick ⚔ on a sector for its ground battle, or ⚔ on the orbit line for the space battle over the system, and that one is yours instead. *Deploy*: a side that took somewhere moves into it and moves what's left, armies to a sector it holds on the same world or next door and ships to an orbit it holds next door, always leaving one behind. *Collect*: income and region bonuses, and taking three or more places in the turn earns a card. Every die and card comes off the campaign's seed, so an online turn replays the same.
- **Cards:** you hold up to five, and play them in the place phase on somewhere you hold. A *weapon upgrade* (crosshair) arms a sector for good: every die its holder rolls defending it is a d8, and a hero battle there gets an extra turret, until it's taken. A *muster* (shield with a plus) raises armies for free, one for every two you already have on that world: two at least, four at most, so it can't snowball. A *trap* (bomb), set on a sector or orbit for the turn, blows up half of whatever attacks it, and everyone of yours there with it: it's for a place you hold with one army or ship. The enemy never sees your hand or your traps. The AI plays its cards on its front line and its traps under a lone army.
- **Orbit → ground:** troops can only land under your own orbit. An enemy orbit blockades a world, so nothing gets built there and supply through it is cut. In the hero battle, your orbit gives you drop-pod reinforcements and orbital strikes, and a fleet of two or more bombards enemy armies every turn. Lose a hero space battle and you feel it at once: the result screen and the log list the armies bombarded below, the blockade, the bombardment to come, and the enemy's troops coming down, often onto a garrison the bombardment already emptied.
- **The muster force:** a side's armies on a world (and the cannons, shields and upgraded guns it holds there) are its muster force, and they feed the space battle over that world: boarding parties go up from it, and the batteries add to a screen or a bombardment. The world's panel shows it on the orbit row (shield with a plus): yours, then theirs, which is a question mark where you have nobody to count it.
- **Ground → orbit:** a shield generator protects its holder's armies from bombardment and cuts the enemy's strikes to one. An anti-ship cannon knocks a ship a turn out of an enemy fleet in its orbit, so a blockade over it doesn't last. On a world with a spaceport, that's where its ships are built. Without the orbit, a side holding a third of a planet or less can't push out from the surface, and has to land again from space.
- **Supply:** supply runs out from your capitals along the hyperlanes, through any system whose orbit the enemy doesn't hold. Armies past an enemy orbit are cut off and lose one a turn, down to a last one holding on.
- **War score:** your share of the key sectors (capitals and spaceports). Hold 75% and you win. Drop under 30% and your income halves, so losing gets worse the longer it goes.
- **The hero battle:** each kind of sector is its own map. Four are in the redwoods of Doren: a shield generator clearing, a capital with three posts and the palace, a spaceport's landing pads and control tower, and a cannon battery. The trunks go up into the haze, sun comes down through the canopy in shafts with dust hanging in the light, ferns are everywhere, and a red dirt trail is worn from post to post. The floor rolls, and rises into wooded banks past the edge of the field. Where the Wooka live, there are huts up the trunks, rope bridges between them, and totems wearing the helmets of Marines who came this way before. The mining canyon is red rock between winding cliffs, and the jungle is a dense stand of thin, vine-hung trees round a sunken temple, with glowing mushrooms in the gloom. The canyon's open sky has Doren's gas giant hanging in it. Every map in the woods is strewn with things to hide behind: rocks, stumps and fallen logs. The Wooka have dug in, with trench lines across their half of the field: earth banked up behind a row of sharpened stakes, now and then a skull on one. Trunks, buildings and rock block movement, sight and shots, and a unit standing close behind one, from where the shot comes, is in cover and takes half as many hits from range. Nothing helps against a club at arm's length. Walls lower than eye height give cover but don't block sight. Each post is a power point: a squat bunker whose core glows in its holder's colour. The defender starts holding the last post, their keep, ringed by turrets, which for the Wooka are rock-chuckers. The Marines field four classes of trooper: assault, heavy (rockets that tear up armour), sniper and engineer (who mends vehicles and turrets). Their vehicles come from the map: tanks and speeders with any army, gunships when the side holds the orbit, and walkers when it holds the world's capital. Gunships fly over everything but can't take a post. The Wooka field a horde of five kinds, with their share of the vehicles coming as yet more Wooka. Fodder are the front line, and at least they are devout. Chubs are bigger, tribal brutes with bigger clubs. Stickies throw spears, then fall back. Boomies run at the thickest knot of the enemy with a lit keg on their backs and go off, hurting their own a little as well, and set each other off. Shanti, the witch doctors, keep behind the line, mend the Wooka round them and throw hexes. The horde hunts in packs: one pack goes up the middle while two swing round the flanks, and when one Wooka is on someone, its pack comes to help. Three on one Marine tear them apart. Things that die come apart in voxel chunks, blasts throw dirt, and dying Marines get a last word in on the radio. Each side has tickets, and the side holding fewer posts bleeds them. Kill enough of the other side (counted in tickets) and your champion takes the field. For the Wooka it's Warbur, a silverback, half Wooka and half bear, a hunched, tusked troll twice a Chub's size. He spears you from afar, then throws his own Wooka at you (E) until they're too beaten up to throw, then goes for your head: a trooper he gets hold of loses it. A spear that lands by a trunk pins you to it, and so do a Sticky's. For the Marines it's Gumpert, who swaps kit with E: a rocket launcher, a stun baton, or a jetpack that carries him over everything. If the hero is on that side, they become the champion; otherwise one walks in at the spawn. The hero's blaster heats as it fires and locks up for a moment if it's run dry, and running straight ahead without firing breaks into a sprint. Hits flash a marker on the crosshair, taking one reddens the edge of the screen, nearby blasts shake the camera, and kills in a row get a toast. Your hero has three lives. Their level carries over between battles, and losing all three lives sidelines them for three turns. You can also leave a battle to the AI from the briefing, or partway through.
- **Match modes:** a ground battle is fought one of five ways. Conquest is the war over every post at once. Assault opens one post at a time, in order: take it, hold it for 40 seconds while they counter-attack, and the next comes live, till the keep falls or the clock runs out. King of the hill puts one live post in the middle of the field and moves it on every 75 seconds, and whoever stands on it drains the other side. Hold-out puts turrets on every post and sends five minutes of waves that keep growing at the defender. Escort puts the attacker's champion on the field from the start, to be walked to the keep: if the hero is attacking, that's you, and an AI champion only moves with a friend close by. Lose the champion and you lose. The HUD says what the mode wants from you right now.
- **The end-of-match vote:** after a ground battle, everyone votes once for how the next one is fought, and every voter's pointer shows on the vote screen as they move it. Against the AI, its pointer wanders over to a card and clicks. In hotseat, the one pointer votes for each side in turn. Online, the other seat's pointer comes over a WebSocket on the campaign's Durable Object. A split vote goes one way by the match id, the same on both screens. A quick-play battle ends in the same vote, and the pick is the rematch.
- **The space battle:** fought by picking, by default. Four rounds, and each round both sides pick one order from a hand of five, seeing the other's only once theirs is in: *broadside* beats boarders and a fleet bombarding, a fighter *screen* beats a broadside and a bombardment, *board* beats a screen and a bombardment and takes prize ships, *bombard* kills armies on the world below but takes every hit, and *pull back* takes half the fire, gives none and comes back harder. No order twice running, so leaning on one gets read. Every hit scales with the fleet's hull, boarding needs troops from the muster force (and a muster of four or more boards harder), and more hull after the last round holds the orbit, the defender on a tie. The enemy's picks come from the AI, which plays either side the same way. A round takes a few seconds, but a bad run loses the orbit. The result carries the hero's picks, and the table fights it again from them.
- **Flying it:** *Fly it* on the briefing puts you in the fighter battle instead, played in 2.5D, looking down at an angle on Doren's orbit, with a faint grid on the battle's plane and a line dropped from each capital ship to it. The ships are built from voxels, like a toy village. Each ship on the map is a capital ship over the planet, launching its wing of fighters. The Marines fly long stepped wedges, black with white trim, rows of lit windows and a command tower astern, with black voxel darts for fighters. The Wooka fly villages on rafts of lashed logs, with huts, a tree, patched hide sails and a carved Wooka head on the bow, and launch hollowed-out logs with hide sails. Cruisers trade broadsides across the gap and shoot at fighters that come close. A third of the fighters fly bombing runs on the enemy cruisers and the rest dogfight. Lose every cruiser and you lose the orbit. At the time limit, the side with more hull left keeps it. Afterwards a cruiser under half its hull counts as lost, and then the turn's landings come down under whoever holds the orbit. The hero flies a fighter of their own, with three lives, like on the ground.

## Run it

```
cd apps/two-fronts
bun install
bun run dev              # in the browser
bun run desktop:dev      # as a desktop app
bun test test            # the rules, fleets, the AI, the turn sequencing, the campaign's Durable Object and both battle sims, headless
bun run edge:dev         # the page plus the Worker and its Durable Objects in local workerd, for online campaigns
```

`bun run dev` alongside `bunx wrangler dev` works too: Vite sends `/api` on to the Worker. `bun run deploy` ships it to Cloudflare. That's the owner's call, as it's a live Worker with Durable Objects on the account.

In a ground battle: click the field to take control, WASD to move, the mouse to look, click to fire, Q for an orbital strike where you're aiming, E to get in or out of a vehicle (as Warbur, to throw a Wooka; as Gumpert, to swap kit), and Esc to let go of the mouse.

In a space battle: your fighter turns towards the mouse. Hold the button to fire, W to boost, S to brake, and the wheel to zoom.

## How it's built

- `src/battle/contract.ts`: `BattleSpec` (what the map gives the battle) and `BattleResult` (what comes back), and `SpaceBattleSpec` and `SpaceBattleResult` for a fight over the orbit. It's the only file both layers import.
- `src/war/`: the strategy layer. It's a deterministic rules engine over plain JSON (`rules.ts`), the three maps (`galaxy.ts`, picked per campaign) and the AI (`ai.ts`). `declareAttacks` turns a turn's moves into the attacks each side is told about, and `diceBattle` is the cannon barrage. `checkRetreats` checks a side's answer to them, and `fogged` is what a side sees of the map. The table holds the moves out of sight while the retreats come in, and the AI answers for its own side (`aiRetreats`). `resolveTurn` pauses on the hero battle's spec. `applyBattleResult` finishes the turn after a ground battle. After a space battle, `applySpaceResult` runs the landings and the ground battles still to come. A turn where somebody took somewhere pauses again for the deploys (`planDeploys` checks them, the AI's come from `aiDeploys`), and `deployTurn` finishes it.
- `src/battle/sim.ts`: the ground battle as a pure fixed-step simulation, scenery and sight lines included. The tests run it from a fixture spec with no strategy layer and no renderer. `view.ts` draws it in three.js and turns the keyboard and mouse into the hero's input. `models.ts` builds the Marines, the Wooka, the vehicles, the woods and the ships from three.js primitives and textures drawn on a canvas, so there are no assets to load or license. Each model is a rig of named joints, its parts merged into a mesh or two per joint (colour, roughness and metalness ride on the vertices), built once and cloned. A Marine is about two dozen draw calls and a Wooka about ten. `animate` poses a clone each frame from what its unit is doing: walk cycles, the Wooka's waddle and club swing, recoil, wobbling antennas, a walker's stride. With `bun run dev`, `/lineup.html` puts every model on a turntable (`?row=marines|wooka|vehicles|space|cruisers`, `&walk=1` to walk them).
- `src/battle/terrain.ts`: the world around a ground battle, for the eye only. It lays the floor, the trees and their boughs, the undergrowth, the sun shafts and motes, the Wooka's huts and totems, and dresses the sim's blocks as sandbags, bunkers or rock. The sim stays a flat plain: `heightAt` lifts what's drawn onto the rolling floor, and none of it changes a shot or a step, so replays still match.
- `src/battle/space-picks.ts`: the pick battle, a pure function of the spec, its seed and the picks. The AI's picker (`aiSpacePick` in `ai.ts`) is handed in, so the battle layer still imports nothing from the war.
- `src/battle/space.ts`: the flown space battle, again a pure fixed-step simulation tested headless. `space-view.ts` draws it in 2.5D: an angled camera from behind your own line that follows the hero's fighter, which steers towards the point on its plane under the mouse.
- `src/battle/replay.ts`: the hero's input, a step at a time, as a log of about 10 to 50 KB that a played result carries. Both sims take their trig from `fmath.ts` (only + − × ÷ and √, which every engine rounds alike), so a log plays back to the same result in any browser, Bun or a Worker.
- `src/war/table.ts`: who sits in each seat and the orders handed in this turn. The last human's orders resolve the turn, and the AI's seat gives its orders then. A hero battle, on the ground or in orbit, holds the turn for that hero's side. It only stores and sequences the pure rules, so the page and the Durable Object run the same code. A result's numbers are never taken at its word: a battle left to the AI is run again, and a played one is played again from its input log, and that run's result is the one kept.
- `src/ui/vote.ts`: the end-of-match vote screen. The vote itself is on the table (`castVote` in `table.ts`), so the page and the Durable Object count it the same way, and the picked mode goes into the next `BattleSpec`.
- `src/edge/`: the Worker (`worker.ts`) and `CampaignRoom`, one Durable Object per campaign. Its SQLite holds the table as one row and every resolved turn as another (`turns`: the orders, the hero battle's result, the log). Each human side gets a secret seat token when the campaign starts. The link carries the token in its `#fragment`, and the object keeps only its hash. A seat sees the map through its own fog of war and who's still to move, never the other side's orders before the turn resolves. While the retreats are out, it sees the declared attacks but not their numbers. `GET /api/campaigns/<id>/history` reads the turns back.
- `src/main.ts`: the map, the orders panel, and the screens between them. A game against the AI or hotseat is saved in the browser's localStorage. An online game lives in its Durable Object (`src/net.ts` calls it).
