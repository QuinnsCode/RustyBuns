# Two Fronts: the lore, from both sides

Everything the game says about the Wooka comes from one of two places: a Thunder Marine telling a story,
or a Wooka at home. The two never agree, and the game never settles it. This is the world those scenes sit
in, so a new story, radio line or cutscene lands in the same one. The words themselves live in
`content/*.json` and the cue lists in `src/battle/cutscenes.ts`; see the README's *Cutscenes* entry for
how a scene is built and when it plays.

## The Wooka

**The briefing version.** Knee-high, round, fluffy, with big black eyes that have nothing behind them and
clubs built to maim. They came with a furry ferocity unseen by the likes of man. Intel called them basically
harmless and has stopped answering. They do not take prisoners: they take antennas, then helmets, then
whatever is left. They hum at night, in harmony, and the humming is at the top of the charts back home. One
ate a moon (a small one, still a moon). They trained the Gale to come when they whistle. One chewed through a
forty-page treaty clause by clause and kept the signatures. They sank a carrier in orbit, and nobody knows
how, because it is orbit. The word for all this, now official and above red on the threat scale, is
**furrocious**: furry and ferocious. Hale came up with it. Hale came up with it quite fast.

**The hearth version.** The Wooka live in the warrens of Doren, the forest moon, in huts up the redwoods
and dens under them, and in the evening they sit in a ring round a fire. The little ones are put to bed
and argue about it. The elder tells a story about the shiny ones and their little box that gets angry and
turns bread black. The Chub, who could throw anyone fifteen metres, carefully does not, and it is the
hardest thing he does all day. Somebody says good night. That is the whole scene. There is no version of a
Wooka evening in which anybody is sharpening anything.

Both are true as far as the teller knows. The Wooka really do hit back, hard, with everything in reach,
and they really do go home afterwards and tuck the small ones in. The Marines have only ever met the first
half.

## The kin

The Wooka a Thunder Marine meets on the field are the same ones round the fire, which the Marines never
work out. *Fodder* are the front line, and at least they are devout. *Chubs* are bigger, with bigger clubs,
and throw their kin for the joy of it (and, at home, do not). *Stickies* throw spears and fall back. *Boomies*
run at the thickest knot of the enemy with a lit keg and go off. *Shanti*, the witch doctors, mend the
Wooka round them and throw hexes; at the hearth the Shanti is the elder with the stories. A *Mossback* is a
Wooka that kept growing, mangy and long in the arm; the radio panics when one shows up. *Warbur*, half
Wooka and half bear, is their champion, and throws his own Wooka at you when the spears run out.

## Doren and the crystal fields

Doren is the forest moon the Wooka call home: redwoods up into the haze, ferns, rope bridges, and totems
wearing the helmets of Marines who came this way before. Under the ferns are the **crystal fields**, the
resource the whole war is over. To the Thunder Marines a crystal field is a strategic asset: it pays for
ships and armies, every world's income comes out of the ground, and High Command wants every field fenced,
sign-posted and held. To the Wooka a crystal field is where you sit in the evening when the fire is out,
because it glows a little and hums back if you hum at it. When the Marines fenced one off for safety (theirs),
the Wooka ate the fence, then the signs, then sat in the field. The Marines filed that as unrest.

## The warrens

A **warren** is a Wooka den and, to a fleet, a hidden landing hold: a place under the canopy where troops
can come down out of the Gale and nobody above can count them. The Wooka know where every warren is,
because they live in them. The Marines find them one at a time, usually by landing on one. A world's
warrens are why a Wooka garrison the fog of war shows as a question mark is never quite the number you
hoped, and why a Marine landing is a beachhead and a Wooka landing is just going home.

## The Gale

**The Gale** is the storm that sweeps across Doren, and the sky over every world the war touches. It
kills anything caught out in the open and reshapes where a fleet can set down. The Wooka read it: they
know three days out, by nose, and when it is due they move the rugs in and shut the warrens. The Marines
have instruments, and fewer dropships. Every Marine story about the Gale ends with a dropship flying into
it; every Wooka scene set before one is about who has to go and get the little one in. The Marines believe
the Wooka trained it, and that it waits for them. The Wooka have not been asked.

## The Thunder Marines

The good guys, the radio keeps saying. Black armour with white trim, full-face helmets with snorkel masks
on the front, and backpacks far too big for anyone, bristling with antennas. They came to clear out a few
knee-high locals and brought walkers, gunships and orbital strikes, and somewhere between the drop and the
generator the hunters became the hunted. They ride down in Drop Two, a dropship that is mostly flying, on
two benches facing each other, and the seat across always has a story. Nobody on Drop Two has met a Wooka
at home. Everybody on Drop Two has a word for them.

## The rule of the running gag

Every scene in the game obeys three rules, and a new one should too:

1. **Every Marine story escalates.** Each ride-down story, radio line and briefing raises the Wooka's
   reputation to a new height, and the Marines take it as plain fact. A story is never about what the
   Thunder Marines did first (they salted the fields, paved the stump, torched the grove): that part is
   said in passing, as orders, standard stuff, and the Wooka's answer is *unprovoked*. Three short lines,
   the bench answers, a dry tag. Deadpan disaster movie: the bones, a very small bite, running away,
   charging, running away again.
2. **Every Wooka scene is domestic.** Fires, bedtime, stories, good night. The subtitle gives the mundane
   truth under the yelling (*He is describing a toaster*). The council is the exception that proves it:
   even there, the Chub throws a little one for fun and nobody asked him to.
3. **The radio never notices.** When a radio line stumbles on the domestic truth (a ring round a fire, a
   very small blanket, screaming that sounds like *again, again*, rugs moved in before the storm), it
   reads it as a war council, a hostage situation, savagery. It never puts two and two together, and
   neither does anyone on the bench.

## House terms

Use these and no others: **crystal fields** (the resource), **warrens** (the Wooka's dens and landing
holds), **the Gale** (the storm), **objective points** (what wins the war), **command cards**, **Doren**
(the forest moon), **Drop Two** (the dropship), **the shiny ones** (what the Wooka call the Marines),
**furrocious** (what the Marines call the Wooka; drawn out to *furrrocious* when someone means it). No
real-world brands, no commercial games or their words, no emojis; lucide icons in any UI.
